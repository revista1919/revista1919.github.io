1. What Type of Article Am I Going to Write?

Step 1: Before you begin, identify what type of article you are going to submit. Each type has specific structure and content requirements.
Article Type What is it? Basic Structure
Original Research Presents new results from your own study (experiment, survey, data analysis) Introduction → Methods → Results → Discussion → Conclusion (IMRaD)
Academic Essay Analyzes a topic with arguments based on bibliographic sources Thesis → Arguments → Counterarguments → Conclusion
Reflective Essay Presents personal reflections on an experience or reading Experience → Personal analysis → Theoretical connection → Learning
Case Report Describes a unique and interesting case (clinical, social, technical) Case presentation → Diagnosis/Analysis → Intervention → Follow-up
Systematic Review Critically summarizes existing literature on a specific question Question → Search methodology → Analysis → Conclusions
Book Review Critical review of a recent academic book Book details → Summary → Critical analysis → Recommendation
1.1 Specific Structures for Each Type

Original Research

  • Introduction: Context, problem, objectives (why did you do this?)
  • Methods: Design, participants, instruments, procedure (how did you do it?)
  • Results: Findings, data, tables, figures (what did you find?)
  • Discussion: Interpretation, comparison with literature, limitations (what does it mean?)
  • Conclusion: Synthesis of contributions and future lines of research

Academic Essay

  • Clear thesis: Your main argument in the first paragraph
  • Development: 3-4 arguments with evidence (citations, data, examples)
  • Counterarguments: Acknowledge and refute opposing positions
  • Conclusion: Reaffirm thesis and propose implications

Case Report

  • Context: Why is this case unique or relevant?
  • Detailed description: Objective data, chronology, observations
  • Analysis: What makes it special? Comparison with literature
  • Lessons learned: What can other professionals/students learn?

Systematic Review

  • Research question: Formulated with PICO criteria (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome)
  • Search strategy: Databases consulted, terms, dates, inclusion/exclusion criteria
  • Flow diagram: How many articles found, excluded, included
  • Synthesis: Summary of findings, quality of evidence, heterogeneity

Book Review

  • Bibliographic record: Author, title, publisher, year, ISBN, pages
  • Objective summary: Main ideas of the book (without unnecessary spoilers)
  • Critical analysis: Strengths, weaknesses, contribution to the field
  • Recommendation: Who is this book useful for?
Remember: If you have doubts about which type corresponds, write to editor@revistacienciasestudiantes.com with a 100-word summary of your idea and we will guide you.

2. Format and Manuscript Preparation

2.1 How Do I Prepare My File Correctly?
  • File format: We only accept Microsoft Word (.docx). If you use Google Docs, go to "File → Download → Microsoft Word (.docx)". If you use LaTeX, export via Pandoc or an online converter.
  • Font: Times New Roman, size 12, 1.5 line spacing (this is standard and facilitates reading)
  • Margins: 2.5 cm on all sides (Word's default setting)
  • Page numbering: Include at the bottom right
How to configure Word?
Open Word → "Layout" tab → "Margins" → "Normal (2.5 cm)"
"Home" tab → select all (Ctrl+A) → choose "Times New Roman", size 12
"Layout" tab → "Paragraph Spacing" → "1.5 lines"
2.2 How Do I Use Headings and Subheadings (Heading Styles)?

What are "Heading Styles"? They are Word's predefined styles that hierarchically structure your document (like a family tree). It is not the same as simply putting text in bold and larger size.

  • Why are they important?
    • The editorial system automatically detects them to create the table of contents
    • They facilitate the reviewer's navigation through the document
    • They help in the conversion to PDF/HTML for final publication
  • How to apply them?
    Steps in Word:
    1. Select the title text
    2. Go to the "Home" tab
    3. In the "Styles" section, choose "Heading 1" (for main sections), "Heading 2" (for subsections), "Heading 3" (for sub-subsections)
  • Example of correct hierarchy:
    Heading 1: Methodology
      Heading 2: Participants
      Heading 2: Instruments
        Heading 3: Satisfaction survey
    Heading 1: Results
Common mistake: Simulating headings with "bold + size 16 + centered". This is not detected by the system and causes technical problems. Use the predefined styles!
2.3 Anonymization for Double-Blind Peer Review

What does "double-blind" mean? Neither the reviewers know who you are, nor will you know who they are. This guarantees impartiality.

  • What should I remove from the manuscript?
    • Your name and co-authors' names (on the first page and in headers/footers)
    • Institutional affiliation (university, school, laboratory)
    • Acknowledgments that reveal identity (tutor, professor, specific scholarship)
    • Names of projects with identifiable funding
    • Self-citations that say "as we showed in our previous study (Pérez 2023)"
  • How do I manage the removed information?
    The two-file rule:
    File A (to send for review): Completely anonymous, without traces of identity
    File B (for you, do not send it): With all complete data

    When the article is ACCEPTED, you will send File B for final publication with all your data.
  • How do I replace self-citations?
    Instead of: "As we demonstrated previously (Pérez and González 2022)..."
    Write: "As has been demonstrated previously ([Author anonymized] 2022)..."

    In the reference list, also replace:
    Before: Pérez, J. and González, M. (2022)...
    After: [Author anonymized]. (2022)...
  • What about file metadata?
    How to remove metadata in Word:
    File → Info → "Inspect Document" → "Inspect" → "Remove All" in document properties and personal information
2.4 The Abstract – Your Gateway

What is it? A brief, self-contained synthesis of your article. It is the main element (and sometimes the only one) that an editor or reviewer reads to decide whether to evaluate your work.

  • Length: Between 150 and 250 words. Excludes bibliographic citations and complex formulas.
  • Language: A version in English and Spanish with faithful technical translation is required.
  • What to include?
    • Context: What is studied and why does it matter? (1-2 sentences)
    • Objective: What were you trying to answer? (1 sentence)
    • Methods: Design, participants, instruments. (1-2 sentences)
    • Results: Key statistical or analytical findings. (2-3 sentences)
    • Conclusion: Implication of your finding. (1 sentence)
Structural Example (Original Research):
"This study examines the effect of classical music on the concentration of university students. Using an experimental design with 60 participants, performance on an attention test with and without background music was compared. Results show a 15% improvement in short-term memory tasks with instrumental music (p < 0.05), although there were no differences in reasoning tasks. It is concluded that ambient music is a viable tool to optimize specific cognitive processes in academic settings."
Example for Book Review:
"This review analyzes 'The Selfish Gene' by Richard Dawkins, evaluating its contribution to science communication and its relevance after four decades. The central concepts proposed by the author are examined, contrasting them with later developments in evolutionary biology. The work stands out for its expository clarity, although it presents limitations in the discussion of altruism and group selection. It is recommended as essential reading for biology students and the general public interested in evolution."
2.5 Length and Limits
  • Recommended maximum length: 10,000 words for the main body of the text (excluding title, abstract, and references).
  • Guideline by article type:

    Length is not a strict limit but a guide to help authors understand the expected depth and scope for each format. The editor reserves the right to suggest cuts if the length is deemed inappropriate for the article type or subject matter.

    • Book Review: 1,000 - 2,000 words
    • Reflective Essay: 1,500 - 3,000 words
    • Case Report: 2,000 - 4,000 words
    • Academic Essay: 3,000 - 6,000 words
    • Original Research: 3,000 - 8,000 words
    • Systematic Review: 4,000 - 10,000 words
  • What counts as a "word" in the main body? The narrative text of the manuscript is considered: introduction, development, results, discussion, and conclusion. Tables and figures are not counted in this main length.
Editor's note: Prioritize clarity and precision over length. A concise, well-structured article will always be better received than a lengthy one that dilutes its central arguments. When in doubt, aim for the middle or lower part of the suggested range for your article type.

3. Citation and References

3.1 What Citation Style Do We Use?

The journal uses the Chicago Manual of Style (17th ed.), Author-Date system. This means:

  • Parenthetical citation in text: Author's last name + year of publication, in parentheses
  • Alphabetical list at the end: All complete references, ordered by last name
Examples of in-text citations:

Paraphrase (you rephrase the idea):
Evolution is a gradual process (Darwin 1859)

Direct quote (you copy verbatim):
"Natural selection works slowly" (Darwin 1859, 78)

Two authors:
(Pérez and González 2022)

Three or more authors:
(Smith et al. 2021)
Examples in the reference list:

Journal article:
Dittmar, Emily L., and Douglas W. Schemske. 2023. "Temporal Variation in Selection Influences Microgeographic Local Adaptation." American Naturalist 202 (4): 471-85. https://doi.org/10.1086/725865.

Book:
Darwin, Charles. 1859. On the Origin of Species. London: John Murray.

Book chapter:
Smith, John. 2020. "Evolutionary Theory Today." In Modern Biology, edited by Ana Pérez, 45-67. Madrid: Scientific Publishing.
Recommendation: Use free bibliographic managers like Zotero or Mendeley. They automatically export in Chicago 17 format. They will save you hours of work and errors.

4. Classification: Keywords and Controlled Vocabularies

4.1 What Are Controlled Vocabularies and Why Do They Matter?

Simple definition: They are official lists of standardized terms that academic databases use to classify and find articles. They are like "official tags" that help your article appear in relevant searches.

  • Why do we ask for them?
    • Your article will be found by other researchers in international databases
    • Guarantees that you use recognized terminology in your discipline
    • Facilitates the assignment of specialized reviewers
  • How do they work? Instead of using your own words, you search the thesaurus for the official term that best describes your topic. For example, instead of "heart attack", MeSH uses "Myocardial Infarction".
4.2 How Do I Search in Each Controlled Vocabulary?

Here we explain step by step how to use each system according to your discipline:

Health and Medicine: MeSH (Medical Subject Headings)

  • What is it? The thesaurus of the U.S. National Library of Medicine
  • How to search?
    1. Go to meshb.nlm.nih.gov/search
    2. Type your term in English (e.g., "heart attack")
    3. The system will show you the official term: "Myocardial Infarction"
    4. Use that exact term (in English) as a keyword
  • Does it have codes? Yes (e.g., D009203), you may use the code associated to each term/li>

Economics: JEL (Journal of Economic Literature)

  • What is it? Classification system of the American Economic Association
  • How to search?
    1. Go to aeaweb.org/econlit/jelCodes.php
    2. Browse by categories (e.g., F - International Economics)
    3. Select the specific code (e.g., F50 - International Relations and International Political Economy)
    4. Enter the alphanumeric code in your submission
  • Does it have codes? Yes, it uses alphanumeric codes (letter + numbers). It is one of the few systems that uses them.

Computer Science: ACM CCS (Computing Classification System)

  • What is it? Classification system of the Association for Computing Machinery
  • How to search?
    1. Go to dl.acm.org/ccs
    2. Use the search engine or browse hierarchical categories
    3. Each term has an associated code (e.g., "Machine learning" = Computing methodologies~Machine learning)
    4. Copy the complete term with its hierarchy
  • Does it have codes? Uses descriptive terms with hierarchy, not simple numerical codes.

Humanities and Social Sciences: UNESCO Thesaurus

  • What is it? Multidisciplinary thesaurus of UNESCO
  • How to search?
    1. Go to vocabularies.unesco.org
    2. Type your term in English
    3. Browse related terms
    4. Use the exact term that appears in the thesaurus
  • Does it have codes? Does not use alphanumeric codes. Only descriptive terms.

Mathematics: MSC 2020 (Mathematics Subject Classification)

  • What is it? Classification system of the American Mathematical Society
  • How to search?
    1. Go to mathscinet.ams.org
    2. Browse by mathematical areas
    3. Each area has specific codes (e.g., 03-XX Mathematical logic and foundations)
    4. Select the most specific code for your topic
  • Does it have codes? Yes, it uses hierarchical alphanumeric codes.

4.3 Keywords
  • How many? 3 to 6 terms
  • In what language? English and Spanish (technical translation)
  • How to choose them?
    • Use specific terms, not general ones (better "multiple linear regression" than "statistics")
    • Combine controlled vocabulary with commonly used terms in your field
    • Think: what words would someone search to find your article?
Example:
English: environmental education; climate change; student perception; school curriculum; sustainable development

Spanish: educación ambiental; cambio climático; percepción estudiantil; currículo escolar; desarrollo sostenible

5. Ethics and Originality

5.1 Plagiarism and Similarity
  • What is plagiarism? Using ideas, text, or data from others without properly citing
  • Similarity limit: Maximum 15% detected by specialized software (Turnitin/iThenticate)
  • How to avoid it?
    • Always cite when you use others' ideas (even if you rephrase them)
    • Use quotation marks for verbatim quotes
    • Truly paraphrase: understand the idea and write it in your own words, don't just change some words
5.2 Use of Artificial Intelligence
  • What is allowed? Use of AI (ChatGPT, Claude, etc.) for:
    • Improving writing and clarity
    • Translating abstracts
    • Suggesting restructuring
    • Correcting grammar
  • What is prohibited?
    • Generating false or simulated data
    • Creating statistical analyses without verification
    • Generating scientific images
    • Writing conclusions without support from your data
  • Should I declare it? Yes, in the cover letter indicate: "ChatGPT was used to improve the writing of the abstract in English" or similar.
5.3 Mandatory Declarations
  • Data Availability: Where is your data? Can others access it?
    Example: "The data supporting this study are available in the institutional repository [URL] or upon reasonable request to the corresponding author."
  • Conflicts of Interest: Did you receive funding? Any interest that could bias?
    Example: "The authors declare no conflicts of interest." or "This study was funded by [scholarship/name]."
  • Use of AI: If you used AI tools, declare it specifically.

7. Editorial Review Flow

Transparency is a pillar of our journal. This is the standard journey of your manuscript from submission to publication.

1

Technical Screening (Desk Review)

The management team verifies anonymity, format, similarity (plagiarism), and thematic focus. If it does not meet the basic requirements, it is returned (Desk Reject).

2

Assignment and Peer Review

A Section Editor assigns the manuscript to 2 or 3 external expert reviewers (double-blind). They evaluate methodological rigor, originality, and contribution to knowledge.

3

Editorial Decision

Based on the reports, the Editor issues a decision:

  • Accept: Goes directly to production.
  • Minor / Major Revisions: You must correct the manuscript and submit a detailed response letter to the reviewers.
  • Reject: Insurmountable methodological weaknesses are notified.
4

Production and Open Publication

The accepted manuscript is typeset, assigned a DOI (Digital Object Identifier), and published under a Creative Commons license (Diamond Open Access, with no APC charges).
